How to Help a Child With 2 Digit Addition With Regrouping

Combine the ones

The child puts the ones from both numbers together. The total is greater than nine, so the ones place can no longer hold every unit as a separate one.

Find a complete group of ten

Ten single units are identified as one complete group. This is the place-value idea behind regrouping: ten ones have exactly the same value as one ten.

Exchange, then add

The child replaces ten ones with one tens rod and moves that new ten to the tens place. The remaining ones stay in the ones place, so every digit keeps its proper value.

Explain the small 1

The final written work now has meaning. The small 1 is not a reminder mark; it represents the new group of ten that the child actually made and moved.

When the ones column makes ten or more, many children know the sum but do not know what should happen next. Regrouping becomes clearer when the extra ten is visible, touchable, and connected to place value.

Ten ones have the same value as one ten

In a two-digit number, each digit has a place-value job. The ones place counts single units. The tens place counts groups of ten. When eight ones and seven ones are combined, there are fifteen ones. Instead of leaving fifteen loose ones in the ones column, trade ten of them for one ten and keep five ones. That is why the written answer has a 5 in the ones place and a new 1 in the tens place.

Base-ten blocks, connecting cubes, coins, or quick drawings all make this exchange visible. Build 28 with two tens and eight ones, then build 17 with one ten and seven ones. Combine the ones first. Circle ten of them and replace the circle with one tens rod. Now the child can see three original tens plus one newly made ten, along with five ones.

The written algorithm should come after the child can explain the exchange. Ask, “What does this small 1 stand for?” A child who answers “one ten” is using regrouping as a concept, not as a copied mark.

Let the child make the trade

Choose an equation with only one regrouping. Have the child build both numbers and count the ones together. When there are ten or more, let the child physically move or draw a group of ten. Then write the exchange beside the equation. The adult can ask questions, but the child should decide when the trade is needed.

Next, remove the materials and draw simple tens sticks and ones dots. Finally, try the same kind of problem with digits only. Moving gradually from objects to drawings to symbols gives the child several ways to understand the same idea.

Use mistakes as clues

If a child writes the two-digit ones sum in the answer without regrouping, return to the ones. Ask whether there are more than nine single units and whether ten of them can become a group. If the child writes the carried 1 in the wrong column, point back to its value: it is one ten, so it belongs with tens.

Check the finished answer with an estimate. Two numbers near thirty and twenty should make a total near fifty. That quick sense check catches many errors without replacing the child’s own reasoning.
