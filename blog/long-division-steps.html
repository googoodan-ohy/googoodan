How to Do Long Division Step by Step With DMSB

Start with equal groups

The child first treats division as sharing, not as a page of unfamiliar marks. Equal groups give the divisor a clear meaning: it tells how large each group is, or how many groups must be made.

Choose a quotient digit

The child asks how many groups fit into the part being considered. Multiplication facts help test the choice. A choice that is too large is useful information, because it shows the child how to adjust.

Record what has been shared

Multiplying and subtracting record the amount already placed into equal groups. Bringing down the next digit adds the amount that is still waiting. Each written line now matches an action the child understands.

Explain and check

The child completes the division, then multiplies the quotient by the divisor. When the product returns to the dividend, the child has evidence that every step fits together.

Remember the DMSB Method

DMSB stands for Divide, Multiply, Subtract, Bring down. Some U.S. classrooms remember the same order with the phrase Does McDonald’s Sell Burgers?

  1. Divide: Decide how many times the divisor fits into the first digit or group of digits.
  2. Multiply: Multiply the quotient digit by the divisor.
  3. Subtract: Subtract that product from the working number.
  4. Bring down: Bring down the next digit from the dividend and repeat.

DMSB is a memory aid, not the whole lesson. Children also need to understand equal groups, place value, and the multiplication relationship behind each step.

Long division is easier to learn when every line has a purpose. This guide begins with a picture of sharing, then connects that picture to the written steps a child sees on a worksheet.

How the Standard Long Division Algorithm Works

Long division has four repeated actions: divide, multiply, subtract, and bring down. They are easier to remember when they are not taught as a chant alone. Divide asks how many equal groups can fit into the part currently being considered. Multiply checks the proposed number of groups. Subtract shows what has not yet been shared. Bring down brings the next amount into the story so the process can continue.

In a division expression, the dividend is the number being divided, the divisor is the number doing the dividing, and the quotient is the result. Naming these parts helps children describe what they are doing instead of memorizing marks on a page.

For example, in 156 ÷ 3, begin with 15 because 3 cannot make a group from the first digit alone. Five groups of 3 make 15. That is why a 5 is written in the quotient, why 5 × 3 is written below, and why subtracting leaves zero. The 6 is then brought down, and 3 fits into 6 two times. The answer, 52, is not a guess; every digit has been checked.

Start with one-digit divisors and exact quotients. A child who can explain the first step aloud is ready for the next line. A child who cannot explain it needs one more concrete sharing example, not a harder page.

Let the child lead one line

Cover all but the first part of the dividend with a finger or scrap of paper. Ask, “How many groups can we make here?” Let the child choose a quotient digit, then use multiplication facts to test it. If the product is too large, the child has useful information and can choose again. This is much more valuable than erasing silently.

After each subtraction, ask what the remainder in that tiny step means. It is the amount still waiting to be shared before the next digit comes down. Repeating this language helps the written algorithm feel logical instead of fragile.

Check without taking over

At the end, multiply the quotient by the divisor. For problems with no remainder, the product should return to the dividend. For problems with a remainder, add that remainder after multiplying. The remainder must always be smaller than the divisor; otherwise there was still enough left to make another group.

When checking a worksheet, do not reveal every answer at once. Ask the child to circle the one line that feels least certain. Compare that line to the answer key together and name which of the four actions needs another try.
