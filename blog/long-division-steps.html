DIVISION GUIDE · SEPTEMBER 15, 2026

How to Practice Long Division Step by Step

Long division becomes less intimidating when a child knows what each line is for. Begin with short, exact-quotient problems and use an answer key to discuss one step at a time.

It is the end of a busy school day, and a child has erased the same line of a long-division problem three times. A parent wants to help, but the page feels different from the way they remember learning. Starting with one clear, printable problem can turn that tense moment into a conversation about the next small step.

Start with a problem the child can explain

Choose a one-digit divisor with no remainder first. A problem such as 156 ÷ 3 lets a child use multiplication facts they already know. Ask, “How many groups of 3 are in 15?” before introducing a shortcut or mnemonic. The goal is not to rush through the page. It is to connect division, multiplication, and subtraction.

Use the same four actions every time

  1. Divide. Decide how many times the divisor fits into the current part of the dividend.
  2. Multiply. Multiply that quotient digit by the divisor.
  3. Subtract. Find what remains in that place.
  4. Bring down. Bring down the next dividend digit and repeat.

For 156 ÷ 3, 3 goes into 15 five times. Write 5, multiply 5 × 3 = 15, subtract to get 0, then bring down 6. Finally, 3 goes into 6 two times. The quotient is 52.

When should remainders come in?

Introduce remainders only after the child can complete several exact-quotient problems without losing the sequence of steps. Explain that the remainder is what cannot be shared equally and that it must be smaller than the divisor. A quick check is useful: multiply the quotient by the divisor, then add the remainder.

Make checking part of the practice

Do not hand over the answer key immediately. Let the child circle the one line that feels uncertain, then compare only that line. This keeps the sheet a learning tool instead of a test. If the same multiplication fact causes several errors, practice that fact separately before printing another division page.
