Three Math Activities for First Graders That Use Paper and Pencils

Touch and count

The child begins with real objects and points to each one once. This builds one-to-one correspondence and connects the counting sequence to an actual quantity.

Organize the quantity

Placing objects in a ten frame makes the amount easier to recognize. Filled and empty spaces reveal number partners without requiring the child to memorize an isolated fact.

Draw what changed

The child represents an action with a quick picture. Objects that join make a group larger; objects that leave make it smaller. The drawing prepares the child to choose an operation.

Say it, then write it

After explaining the situation aloud, the child writes a number sentence. Symbols come last, once the quantity and the change already make sense.

First grade math becomes more meaningful when a child can touch, draw, and explain quantities before being asked to write a number sentence. These short activities turn early practice into a small discovery.

A number is more than a numeral

A first grader may be able to say the counting sequence while still finding it difficult to see that the last number counted tells “how many.” Pointing to one object at a time helps build that connection. After counting two groups, ask which has more, which has fewer, or whether they are the same. Let the child explain by matching objects one-to-one before using comparison symbols.

Ten frames are especially useful because they organize a quantity without changing it. Seven counters on a ten frame show seven filled spaces and three empty spaces. The child can see 7 + 3 = 10, not just hear it. This visual relationship later supports addition within 20 and the idea of making a new ten.

Picture stories connect quantities to operations. If six apples are on a table and four more are added, the total grows. If ten cookies are on a plate and three are eaten, the group gets smaller. Encourage the child to draw the starting amount and the change before choosing a plus or minus sign.

Try three five-minute activities

Count and compare: Place two small groups of buttons or crayons on the table. Count each group, line them up to compare, then write the numbers. Ask the child to tell you what they notice before you introduce a symbol.

Build ten: Draw a ten frame or use a printable one. Put in a small number of counters and ask how many empty spaces remain. Switch the colors and repeat. The goal is to recognize partners that make ten, not to race.

Tell a picture story: Draw a few simple objects, then add or cross out some of them. Ask the child to explain the beginning, the change, and the ending. Only after the story is clear should the child write an equation.

Notice the idea, not just the answer

A correct answer is useful, but the explanation matters more. If a child says there are more in one group, ask how they know. If the child fills a ten frame, ask what the empty spaces mean. These small questions reveal whether the child is counting, comparing, or guessing.

End while the activity is still pleasant. Repeating a familiar activity with new numbers builds confidence and helps the child return willingly tomorrow.
