Decimals: practice notes

Decimal work becomes clearer when each digit is connected to a place-value unit. Read 3.47 as three ones, four tenths, and seven hundredths, then use that meaning to compare or calculate. In addition and subtraction, line up decimal points so tenths meet tenths and hundredths meet hundredths; an empty place can be written as zero without changing the value. In multiplication, estimate first because the product of two numbers less than one is usually smaller than either factor. Estimation gives a quick check on where the decimal point belongs.

Select a page by the operation and the place values it uses. Comparing decimals asks learners to compare tenths, hundredths, or thousandths; equivalent forms may require adding zeros; rounding asks which digit controls the chosen place. Division introduces a different question: how many groups fit, and how should the quotient be rounded? Keep the units visible while working. A learner who writes 0.4 as 0.04 may understand the operation but confuse tenths and hundredths, so return to a place-value chart before assigning more calculations.

A steady sequence is to read and model the numbers, estimate the result, calculate, and compare the answer with the estimate. For subtraction, check that regrouping across a zero keeps the value of every place. For division, verify by multiplying the quotient by the divisor. Use the answer key to spot a repeated place-value error rather than correcting each answer in isolation. The decimal worksheets vary the operation, number of decimal places, and layout; choose one change at a time so the learner can focus on the idea being practised.
