Telling Time: practice notes

Time questions require a clear starting point and unit. An analog clock?s short hand shows the hour and the long hand shows minutes; when the minute hand moves, the hour hand also moves gradually toward the next number. Counting by fives helps read the minute marks, while a digital display shows hours and minutes directly. Ask learners to say both forms aloud and notice that 3:45 is fifteen minutes before four, not forty-five minutes after four in every context.

Elapsed-time problems ask how much time passes between two moments. Draw a number line or clock and move first to the next hour, then count the remaining minutes. For example, from 2:50 to 3:20 is ten minutes to three plus twenty more, or thirty minutes. This method is often clearer than subtracting digits in a clock display. When the question uses a.m. or p.m., identify whether the interval crosses noon or midnight before calculating.

Start with exact hour and half-hour readings, then add quarter hours and five-minute intervals. A common mistake is to read the hour hand as if it jumps instantly when the minute hand reaches twelve; it moves continuously. Another is to subtract 50 from 20 without accounting for the hour boundary. Have the learner draw or describe the movement and check whether the interval makes sense in the story. The time worksheets move between clock faces, digital notation, and elapsed-time reasoning; choose one representation at a time when a new skill is introduced.
