Place Value: practice notes

Place value explains why the same digit can represent different amounts. In 4,206, the four means four thousands, the two means two hundreds, and the six means six ones. Ask learners to read each digit with its unit, then expand the number as a sum. A place-value chart makes the columns visible and helps connect standard, word, and expanded forms. For decimals, extend the chart to tenths and hundredths; the places to the right of the decimal point represent parts of one whole.

Choose practice by the representation the learner needs. Writing a number from a chart builds column awareness; comparing numbers develops attention to the greatest place that differs; composing or decomposing numbers supports mental calculation. When adding or subtracting, align digits by place rather than by the edge of the page. Appending a zero to a decimal can show equivalent value, but moving the decimal point changes the number unless the unit is changed too. Make these distinctions explicit before using shortcuts.

A helpful check is to estimate the number?s size and read it aloud with place names. If an answer is ten times too large or too small, revisit the place columns. When regrouping, explain that ten ones become one ten and ten tens become one hundred; the quantity stays the same while its representation changes. The place-value worksheets vary the number range and format. Start with a chart or expanded form, then use more compact notation after the learner can explain what each digit contributes.
