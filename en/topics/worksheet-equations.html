Equations: practice notes

An equation states that two expressions have the same value. The equals sign does not mean ?write the answer next?; it means both sides balance. When a number is missing, read the equation as a relationship. In 7 + ? = 12, ask what must be added to seven to reach twelve. A number line can show the distance, while a related subtraction equation gives 12 ? 7. These are two views of the same fact. Encourage the learner to check a proposed value in the original equation, not only in the rearranged step.

Missing-number pages can vary in useful ways: the unknown may be an addend, a starting number, a factor, or a quotient. Identify the operation and the location of the blank before calculating. For addition and multiplication, inverse operations often reveal the unknown; for subtraction, pay attention to whether the blank is the first or second number because the order changes the meaning. More complex equations may require two steps, but it is still helpful to preserve equality at each step by doing the same operation to both sides.

Begin with a small set of one-step examples and ask the learner to explain why the answer makes both sides equal. Then try a different blank position or operation while keeping the numbers simple. A common error is to perform an operation on only one side; another is to treat 9 ? ? = 4 as 9 ? 4 without explaining why. Use substitution to check: replace the box and evaluate each side. The worksheet collection supports practice with balanced equations, missing values, and simple relationships, allowing instruction to move from concrete reasoning toward efficient calculation.
