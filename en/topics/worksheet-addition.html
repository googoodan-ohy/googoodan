Addition: practice notes

Addition practice works best when the learner can explain what each number represents. Start with a short set of facts or pictures, then move to missing addends, number lines, and two-digit calculations as the examples become familiar. For a regrouping page, ask the learner to group ten ones before writing a ten in the next place. For a non-regrouping page, have them estimate first and check whether the sum fits the stated range. These prompts help distinguish a calculation error from a place-value misunderstanding.

When choosing a worksheet, look at the size of the addends, whether the page uses horizontal or vertical layout, and whether the unknown appears at the beginning, middle, or end of an equation. A child who can solve 8 + 5 by counting on may be ready to make ten; someone who already decomposes 8 + 5 into 8 + 2 + 3 can try related facts. Use the answer key to find patterns in mistakes, such as counting the starting number twice or carrying a digit without regrouping the ones.

A useful sequence is one worked example, a few questions solved aloud, and then a small independent set. Keep the same number range while changing the representation, such as moving from counters to a number line. Once the method is reliable, change one feature at a time: larger numbers, a missing addend, or regrouping. Stop before practice turns into guessing. The addition collection includes several formats, so families and teachers can choose a page that matches the current strategy and revisit it later with fresh values.
